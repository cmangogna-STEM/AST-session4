Rainforest Ecosystem & Colonization as Root Cause
Grades 5–6 · San Francisco · Science & Social Studies Integration
5th–6th Grade
The Phenomenon & Unit Structure
Students investigate why rainforest ecosystems in Eritrea (East Africa) are disappearing. The anchoring phenomenon: satellite images showing forest loss over decades.

  • Stage 1: Students notice patterns in satellite data, generate questions about causation
  • Stage 2: Elicitation — "Why do you think this is happening?" draws out initial ideas
  • Stage 3: Investigation into ecosystems, biodiversity, soil, and water cycles
  • Stage 4: A 6th grader connects colonization and resource extraction as root causes of deforestation — not just population growth
"A student raises their hand: 'But why is Eritrea so poor that they have to cut the trees? Who took the resources before?'"
— Classroom vignette, 6th grade San Francisco
Justice-Centered Elements
  • Community knowledge as data: Students bring family stories about land use and agriculture
  • Historical root causes: Colonization is framed as a driver of environmental degradation, not background context
  • Agency: Unit ends with students drafting letters to conservation organizations, naming both ecological AND political solutions
  • Indigenous knowledge: Students study Afar pastoralists' traditional land management practices as expert ecological knowledge
Hindou Oumarou Ibrahim Connection
"We indigenous peoples have a PhD in sustainable reforestation and land management. We protect 80% of the remaining biodiversity." — This is the expert knowledge the unit centers.
City Design Engineering — Who Gets the Cool Spaces?
Grades 1–3 · Urban School · Engineering Integration & Biomimicry
Grades 1–3
The Phenomenon & Unit Structure
Students investigate why their school playground is hotter than the park two blocks away. Anchoring phenomenon: students measure temperatures at multiple spots in the neighborhood.

  • Stage 1: Neighborhood temperature walk — students become data collectors
  • Stage 2: Initial models — "Why does that area stay cooler?"
  • Stage 3: Investigation into albedo, thermal mass, evapotranspiration, shade
  • Stage 4: Design engineering challenge — redesign the school grounds to reduce the heat island effect using biomimicry principles
Justice-Centered Elements
  • Local, consequential phenomenon: Students study their own school — not a distant ecosystem
  • Structural analysis: Class investigates: "Why does our neighborhood have less tree canopy than wealthy neighborhoods?" — this is the justice move
  • Biomimicry as justice: Student designs draw on nature's cooling strategies — connecting to the idea that nature holds solutions humans have overlooked
  • Real audience: Students present designs to the principal and school board — their work is real civic action
  • Eco-emotions managed: "The problem is big, but your design is real" frames hope as discipline
"A 2nd grader: 'We should put trees in the parking lot. The butterfly would come back and it would be cooler for us to play AND for them.'"
— Student design presentation, urban school
